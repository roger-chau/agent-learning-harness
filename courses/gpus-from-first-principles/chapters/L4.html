Why this lab

"I wrote an SGEMM that reaches X% of cuBLAS, and here's the profile of each step" is one of the strongest things a GPU performance candidate can say. It's also the most widely read tutorial genre in the field, because it exercises everything at once: memory hierarchy, data reuse, registers, occupancy and instruction mix. This lab walks Part 4's ladder on your card, against cuBLAS, ending on the tensor cores.

What to do

Open labs/L4-gemm/l4.cu. All matrices are 2048 × 2048, row-major. Implement four kernels:

  1. Naive: one thread per output.
  2. Tiled: 32 × 32 shared-memory tiles (Chapter 4.2).
  3. Register-tiled: 256 threads compute a 128 × 128 tile, 8 × 8 outputs each, with BK = 8. Store A's tile transposed so each thread's 8 values of a column are contiguous, and read both operands with float4 (Chapters 4.3 and 4.4). The kernel has __launch_bounds__(256): check with make regs that it doesn't spill.
  4. Tensor cores with WMMA: 4 warps per block computing a 64 × 64 tile, each warp a 32 × 32 sub-tile as 2 × 2 fragments of 16 × 16 × 16, FP16 inputs staged in padded shared memory, FP32 accumulators (Chapter 4.5).

The inputs are chosen so every product and partial sum is exact in FP32 (and the inputs are exact in FP16), so your results must match cuBLAS's essentially to the bit. A mismatch is a bug, not rounding.

Predict, then measure

  • Look up your card's FP32 and FP16 tensor-core peak throughput. What fraction does cuBLAS reach?
  • For each kernel, estimate global loads per FMA and shared loads per FMA (Chapters 4.1–4.3). Which kernel should be bound by what?
  • Profile reg with make ncu: issue slot utilisation, achieved occupancy (it's low by design: why is that fine?), and the warp stall reasons.

Going further

  • Double buffering: load the next K-step's tiles into registers while computing the current one (Turing has no cp.async; that's an Ampere feature, in the cloud labs).
  • Warp tiling: arrange the 256 threads so each warp covers a 64 × 32 region, and check bank conflicts on the shared reads with Lab 2's counters.
  • WMMA, bigger: 128 × 128 block tiles, 8 warps, each warp 64 × 32 (4 × 2 fragments), and a K-step of 32. The gap to cuBLAS's FP16 GEMM is the subject of CUTLASS's design.
  • Keep a log of every change with its TFLOP/s: it becomes your portfolio write-up.

Passing

All four kernels match cuBLAS; tiled at least 1.3× faster than naive; your register-tiled SGEMM at least 50% of cuBLAS SGEMM; your WMMA kernel at least 1.3× faster than your register-tiled one and at least 25% of cuBLAS's FP16 GEMM. The report is the optimisation log.
