The real problem

A GPU's peak numbers assume a clock it can't always hold. Run a heavy tensor-core workload and the board hits its power limit, the clock drops, and your measured speed falls. In a datacenter, the electricity bill and the cooling are the constraint; on a battery-powered robot or a Jetson-class board, the power budget is the spec. Performance per watt is the number that decides hardware purchases and embedded designs.

The smallest idea: power is capacitance, voltage squared, frequency

$$P_{\text{dynamic}} \approx C\, V^2 f$$

To run at a higher frequency, a chip needs a higher voltage, so dynamic power grows roughly with the cube of the clock. Add static (leakage) power, paid whenever the chip is on. DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) moves the clock and voltage together, many times a second, to stay within power and temperature limits: that's the "boost clock" and its opposite, throttling (H&P Chapter 1).

The consequence for kernels: a memory-bound kernel gains almost nothing from a faster core clock (DRAM sets the time) but pays the power, so running it at a lower clock saves energy for free. A compute-bound kernel trades time against power, and its energy-optimal clock is where static and dynamic costs balance.

Add one block: moving data costs more than math

Energy per operation, in a widely cited estimate for a 45 nm process (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014): a 32-bit floating-point multiply about 3.7 pJ, an add about 0.9 pJ, a 32-bit read from a small on-chip SRAM about 5 pJ, and a 32-bit read from off-chip DRAM about 640 pJ. Newer processes shrink the logic numbers faster than the DRAM one.

So reading an operand from DRAM costs over a hundred times more energy than computing with it. Every technique in this course that cut DRAM traffic (coalescing, tiling, fusion, FlashAttention) saved energy as well as time, often more energy than time. Hardware designers made the same argument for matrix units and systolic arrays (the TPU paper).

The whole machine: clocks in practice

  • Datacenter GPUs expose power limits and application clocks (nvidia-smi). Many sites cap power below the maximum because performance per watt improves.
  • Embedded boards offer power modes (e.g. Jetson's nvpmodel) that fix clocks and active units for a power budget, and thermal limits can throttle further when the enclosure heats up.
  • Benchmarks: profilers often lock clocks to base frequency for repeatable numbers (Chapter 5.1); users run at boost. A "20% speedup" measured at locked clocks may shrink, or grow, in the field.

Reality

  • Power is measured per board, coarsely. Attribute energy to kernels with a model (counts × energy per event), like the one in the exercise.
  • Thermal behaviour has time constants of seconds: a 50 ms benchmark may never throttle, while the same kernel in a 10-minute job will.
  • For embedded vision pipelines, frames per joule is often a better target than frames per second.

Worked examples

Example 1: reading a power-limited profile

Measurements for a large FP16 GEMM: 72% of the tensor-core peak computed at the boost clock; SM clock reported during the run 1.55 GHz against a 1.98 GHz boost; board power pinned at the 350 W limit.

Recompute the peak at the actual clock: 1.55/1.98 = 78% of the boost peak. So the kernel achieves 72/78 = 92% of what the hardware can do at the clock it's allowed to run. The remaining gap to the spec sheet is power, not code. Reporting "72% of peak" without the clock misleads both ways: it undersells the kernel and oversells the hardware.

Example 2: choosing a power mode for an edge device

Setup. A detector needs 30 fps. At the 25 W mode it runs at 55 fps drawing 22 W; at the 15 W mode 34 fps drawing 13 W.

Energy per frame if each runs flat out: 22/55 = 0.40 J versus 13/34 = 0.38 J, nearly equal. But the job needs only 30 fps, and idle power still counts. The 25 W mode is busy 30/55 = 55% of the time at 22 W and idles the rest at about 5 W: average 14.3 W, or 0.48 J per frame. The 15 W mode is busy 88% of the time at 13 W and idles at about 4 W: average 11.9 W, 0.40 J per frame. The lower mode wins by about 16%, and runs cooler with no throttling risk. "Race to idle" only wins when idle power is very low.

Example 3: designing a fair benchmark protocol

  1. Record GPU model, driver, CUDA version, power limit and application clocks.
  2. Lock clocks where possible (nvidia-smi -lgc); otherwise warm up until clocks and temperature stabilise (Lab 9 shows how long that takes on your card).
  3. Interleave A and B, many repetitions; report medians and spread.
  4. Report both time and energy (NVML energy counter) for any comparison where the kernels differ in arithmetic intensity: a "faster" kernel that pins the power limit can lose to a slightly slower one in throughput per watt.
  5. State whether caches start warm or cold.
