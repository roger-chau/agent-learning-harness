Why this lab

In a transformer, the matmuls get the headlines, but the operators between them decide a surprising share of the time: softmax, LayerNorm, activations and residual adds. All of them are memory-bound, so the only questions are how many times you touch the data and how close to peak bandwidth each touch runs. Around the whole model sits the host: inputs come in over PCIe and results go back, and a serving system that doesn't overlap those copies with compute leaves the GPU idle. Writing fused kernels for these operators is a large part of what ML-systems kernel teams do.

What to do

Open labs/L7-workloads/l7.cu. The matrices are 32,768 rows × 1,024 columns.

  • A. Softmax. The given version is three reasonable kernels, each with a block per row. Count its passes over the matrix and predict the time. Then write softmax_fused, with one warp per row. Each lane holds its 32 values in registers, loaded as float4s strided by the warp so the loads coalesce. The kernel computes the max, then the sum of exponentials, then writes once. Subtracting the max isn't optional: work out what expf(80) is in FP32.
  • B. LayerNorm. Same structure, with mean and variance. Use the two-pass formula on the values already in registers. That costs no extra memory traffic, and it avoids the cancellation in E[x²] − E[x]² (Chapter 9.1).
  • C. The pipeline. The given run_serial copies 128 MiB up from ordinary host memory, runs a kernel, and copies it back. The program calibrates the kernel to take about as long as the upload. Write run_pipelined: pinned buffers (already allocated), 16 chunks issued round-robin on 4 streams, each chunk's copy up, kernel and copy down on its own stream. Predict the speedup from the number of copy engines your card reports. Then look at the timeline with make nsys.

Predict, then measure

  • A: the unfused softmax moves 5 matrices' worth of bytes and yours moves 2. What speedup does that predict, and why might you measure less?
  • A and B: 32 values per lane means 64 or more registers per thread. What occupancy is left (make regs), and is it enough to saturate memory? Use Little's law with your card's latency from Lab 0.
  • C: with one copy engine, uploads and downloads take turns. With two, they overlap too. Draw the timeline you expect for each case before running nsys.

Going further

  • Online softmax: one pass that keeps a running max and rescales the running sum (Milakov & Gimelshein, 2018). You'll need it for Lab 8. Implement it for rows too long to fit in registers, say 65,536 columns with a block per row.
  • LayerNorm with Welford's algorithm, merging the statistics across lanes with shuffles. Compare its accuracy with the two-pass version on rows with mean 10⁴ and standard deviation 1.
  • Fuse a residual add and a bias into the LayerNorm, as real transformer kernels do: what does it save?
  • C: vary the number of chunks from 2 to 64. Why does it get worse at both ends?

Passing

All results correct. Your softmax and LayerNorm each reach at least 70% of peak bandwidth, and the fused softmax is at least 2× faster than the unfused one. The pipeline is at least 1.4× faster than the serial version. The report includes the nsys timeline of the pipeline, with the overlap marked.
