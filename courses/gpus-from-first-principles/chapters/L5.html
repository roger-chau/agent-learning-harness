Why this lab

Part 5 is about measuring before guessing. On a real card that means three skills: knowing your machine's actual limits (not the spec sheet's), knowing what a kernel should take before you profile it, and reading Nsight Compute well enough to name the bottleneck in a minute. This lab builds all three. You measure your card's roofline from first principles, watch a tail effect cost a whole wave, and then fix a slow kernel starting from its profile.

What to do

Open labs/L5-roofline/l5.cu.

  • A. FP32 peak. In fma_peak, give each thread 8 independent FMA chains, so that both warps and instruction-level parallelism hide the 4-cycle FMA latency (Chapter 6.4 has the theory; here you just need it to run at peak). The program compares your rate with the theoretical one: SMs × 64 lanes × 2 FLOPs × clock. Under GPU Boost your card runs above its reported clock, so above 100% is possible.
  • B. The roofline. intensity<K> streams a 256 MiB array and applies K FMAs to each element: K / 4 FLOP per byte. The program sweeps K from 1 to 1024 and writes roofline.csv. Plot GFLOP/s against FLOP/byte on log–log axes, and draw your two measured ceilings on top. Before you run it, predict the K where the kernel stops being memory-bound (Chapter 2.1's ridge point, from your card's specifications).
  • C. Tail effects. busy blocks all take the same time. Write wave_capacity with cudaOccupancyMaxActiveBlocksPerMultiprocessor. The program times one wave, one wave plus one block, and two waves, then the same total work as one wave plus one block, cut into blocks 8× smaller (Chapter 5.4). Predict all four times first.
  • D. Diagnose, then fix. gemv_slow is a correct and very slow matrix–vector product. Before changing anything, run make ncu-gemv and write down three numbers: DRAM throughput as a percentage of peak, global-load sectors per request, and achieved occupancy. Explain each from the code. Then write gemv_fast and gemv_grid. One warp per row, reading float4s and finishing with a shuffle reduction, is the standard design. Profile it again and compare.

Reading the profile

  • Speed of light: compute and memory throughput as a percentage of peak. If both are low, the kernel is latency-bound: not enough work in flight.
  • Memory workload analysis: sectors per request (4 is ideal for 4-byte loads, 32 is the worst case), and L1 and L2 hit rates. gemv_slow's L1 hit rate is high, and it doesn't save it. Why not?
  • Occupancy and launch statistics: how many warps the grid even has, compared with what the card can hold. 16,384 rows at one thread per row is 512 warps for 34 SMs.
  • Warp state statistics: the top stall reason, such as "long scoreboard" (waiting on memory) or "math pipe throttle".
  • make roofline draws Nsight Compute's own roofline for part B. Check that it agrees with your plot.

Going further

  • Replace busy's clock spin with a compute loop (dependent FMAs) and repeat C. The lone tail block now runs faster than a block sharing its SM. Work out how much faster from the issue rates.
  • Fix C's tail without changing the work per block: a persistent kernel with an atomic work counter. When does it help, and when do smaller blocks win?
  • GEMV at different shapes: 256 rows × 262,144 columns, and 1,048,576 rows × 64 columns. One warp per row is wrong for both. Design a mapping for each.
  • Lock your clocks (nvidia-smi -lgc, if your driver allows it) and see how much run-to-run variance drops (Chapter 5.1).

Passing

All checks pass. FMA rate at least 75% of theoretical. The K = 1 kernel reaches at least 70% of peak bandwidth, and the K = 1024 kernel at least 70% of part A's FMA rate. One extra block costs at least 1.7× a wave, and the finer split brings it back under 1.3×. Your GEMV reaches at least 70% of peak bandwidth and is at least 3× faster than the given one. The report has the roofline plot and the before-and-after profile numbers.
