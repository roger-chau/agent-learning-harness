The real problem

"It's slow" isn't a diagnosis. Before changing a line of code, a performance engineer names the bottleneck and points at the evidence. The fix follows from the name: you don't coalesce a compute-bound kernel, and you don't unroll a latency-bound one. Most wasted optimisation effort comes from skipping this step.

The smallest idea: five kinds of slow

Every kernel is limited by one of a few things, and the metrics from Chapter 5.2 tell you which.

DiagnosisEvidenceTypical fixes
Launch-boundKernel time well under the wall time per launchFuse kernels, batch work, CUDA Graphs
Latency-boundEvery SOL low (here: busiest unit under 60%)More warps, more ILP, more work in flight, double buffering
Bandwidth-bound (DRAM or L2)That memory's SOL highFewer bytes: coalescing, reuse, tiling, fusion, smaller types
Compute-boundIssue SOL high, mostly FMAs (or tensor SOL high)Better algorithm, tensor cores, lower precision. Or celebrate
Issue-boundIssue SOL high, mostly not FMAsFewer instructions per useful op: register tiling, vector loads, no bank conflicts, less divergence

Add one block: the procedure

  1. Launch check. If the kernel's own time is less than half the wall time per launch, stop: it's launch-bound.
  2. Find the busiest unit among DRAM, L2, issue and tensor SOL.
  3. If it's below 60%, nothing is saturated: latency-bound. Check occupancy and ILP (Little's law).
  4. Otherwise it's that unit. For issue, split by FMA share: at least half FMAs means compute-bound, otherwise issue-bound. Then find out which instructions: bank conflicts (wavefronts), branches, address arithmetic, barriers.

The 60% line is a convention, not a law. Near it, a kernel is usually a mix, and the honest diagnosis says so.

The whole machine: the bottleneck moves

Fixing the bottleneck exposes the next one. Part 4 was this loop: naive GEMM (issue-bound on loads), tiled (issue-bound on shared loads), register-tiled (compute-bound), tensor cores (latency-bound), double-buffered (issue-bound again, at 2.7× the FP32 roof). Each step's diagnosis chose the next step's fix. Keep a log, as you did in C3.

Reality

  • Real kernels have phases: a load phase that's bandwidth-bound and a compute phase that's issue-bound. Whole-kernel averages hide that; source-level profiling (per-instruction stall sampling) shows it.
  • "Latency-bound" on real hardware also covers dependency chains within a thread, not just memory. The fix is the same: more independent work.
  • Write the diagnosis down with its numbers before you change anything. That's what B5 asks for.

Worked examples

Example 1: a full diagnosis write-up

Kernel: a 3 × 3 convolution on a 4096 × 4096 image, 32 × 8 blocks, 1.9 ms on the GW-1.

  1. Launch check: one launch, 1.9 ms: not launch-bound.
  2. SOLs: DRAM 26%, L2 48%, issue 71% (FMA share 35%), tensor 0.
  3. Busiest unit: issue at 71%, above 60%: issue-bound, FMA share under half, so overhead.
  4. Which instructions? 9 global loads per output (each 4 bytes), 18 instructions of clamp arithmetic, 9 FMAs: loads and clamps dominate.
  5. Fix: stage a (32 + 2) × (8 + 2) tile in shared memory with one coalesced load per element, pad the image instead of clamping, and compute 2 outputs per thread to reuse loaded neighbours in registers.
  6. Prediction: instructions per output from about 40 to about 15: issue time 2.7× lower; DRAM (8 bytes per pixel minimum, 0.26 ms at peak) becomes the next limit at roughly 0.4–0.5 ms.

Written this way, the change is justified before it's made, and the prediction can be checked afterwards.

Example 2: launch-bound in disguise

Symptom: an inference step runs 400 small kernels in 6 ms; Nsight Systems shows the GPU busy for only 2.4 ms.

Diagnosis: each kernel averages 6 µs of GPU time and the host issues one every 15 µs: the GPU is idle 60% of the time. No kernel-level optimisation helps. Fixes: CUDA Graphs (record the 400 launches once, replay with one call: per-launch overhead drops to ~1 µs), and fusing adjacent elementwise kernels. Expected: 2.4 ms + 400 × ~1 µs ≈ 2.8 ms, a 2× end-to-end improvement from code that didn't change any kernel.

Example 3: a bottleneck that moves twice

Start: a reduction at 30% of bandwidth, latency-bound (all SOLs low, occupancy 25%).

Fix 1, 4 independent loads per thread: 85% of bandwidth. DRAM-bound now.

Fix 2, "fewer bytes": the input is FP32 but only needs FP16 precision. Converting it upstream halves the bytes: time halves, still DRAM-bound at 85%.

Fix 3? Nothing left within this kernel: it reads each byte once at near-peak bandwidth. The remaining lever is fusion with the producer kernel (so the data is reduced as it's produced, never written). Knowing when a kernel is done is as important as knowing how to speed it up.
