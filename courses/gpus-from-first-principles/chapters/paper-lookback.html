The paper
D. Merrill, M. Garland. "Single-pass Parallel Prefix Scan with Decoupled Look-back." NVIDIA Technical Report NVR-2016-002, 2016.
Find it on Google Scholar · Short, and the algorithm section is the heart of it.

The problem

Chapter 3.2 left scan at about $3n$ memory traffic (reduce-then-scan) or worse. A copy moves $2n$, so classic GPU scans ran at most around two-thirds of the speed of simply copying the data. The obvious single-pass alternative, chained scan, has each tile wait for its predecessor's inclusive prefix before publishing its own. That makes the whole scan a serial chain of tile latencies: tile 1000 can't finish until tiles 0–999 have finished one after another.

The key idea

Decouple a tile's own total from its inclusive prefix, and let successors look back over as many predecessors as needed. Each tile has a status word with three states:

  • X: nothing published yet;
  • A: the tile's aggregate (its own total) is available;
  • P: the tile's inclusive prefix (everything up to and including it) is available.

A tile computes its aggregate locally and publishes A immediately, without waiting for anyone. Then it walks backwards, summing the aggregates of A tiles, until it reaches a P tile, whose prefix ends the walk. It publishes P for itself, and successors that reach it stop there. A tile only waits on predecessors that haven't published anything, and that window is short because every tile publishes A right after its local reduction.

Two practical details make it work on real hardware:

  • Dynamic tile ids from an atomic counter, so a tile only waits on tiles that have already started. Hardware doesn't promise to schedule blocks in blockIdx order. Without this, a tile could wait for one that never gets scheduled because the waiting tiles occupy every SM: a deadlock.
  • Status and value published together, packed into one word or ordered with a memory fence, so a reader never sees a flag without its value.

The result: a scan that reads the input once and writes the output once, about $2n$, running at roughly copy speed.

Reading guide

  1. Background on parallel scan strategies. Read the comparison of reduce-then-scan, scan-then-propagate and chained scan, with their data movement. Check it against Chapter 3.2.
  2. The decoupled look-back algorithm. Read very closely, with the X/A/P states. Trace a small example by hand with four tiles finishing in a scrambled order.
  3. Implementation details: how status and value are packed, the fences, and the dynamic tile ids. Read closely; these are exactly the pitfalls milestone B2 makes you face.
  4. Performance results. Skim, noting the comparison with memcpy throughput.

Reproduce it

In the exercise you'll model the two things the paper argues about:

  • Data movement for each strategy, as a function of $n$.
  • A single look-back: given the status of the tiles before yours, compute your exclusive prefix and how many predecessors you inspected, or report that you must wait.

Then compare your B2 kernel's dramBytes with the model's $2n$.

What happened next

Decoupled look-back is the scan inside CUB (and therefore Thrust and many frameworks). The same "publish early, look back" pattern now appears wherever a single pass needs a running total across blocks: stream compaction, run-length encoding, and radix sort. This Part's next paper, Onesweep, uses it to make each radix-sort pass a single sweep over the keys.
