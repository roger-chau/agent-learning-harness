The signal

"Expected number of…", "expected value of a game", "how much would you pay to play?". Expectation questions are the backbone of quant interviews because pricing is expectation. The tool that makes most of them easy is linearity with indicator variables.

The core idea: E[sum] = sum of E, even for dependent terms

Write the count you want as a sum of indicators I₁ + … + Iₙ, where Iₖ = 1 if "thing k happens". Then E[count] = Σ P(thing k happens). No independence is needed: that's what makes linearity so powerful.

  • Fixed points of a random permutation: Σ P(σ(i) = i) = n × 1/n = 1.
  • Distinct faces in 6 rolls: Σ over faces of P(face appears) = 6(1 − (5/6)⁶) ≈ 3.99.
  • Coupon collector: the time to collect all n coupons is a sum of geometric waits with means n/n, n/(n − 1), …, n/1, so E = n·Hₙ (14.7 for n = 6).
  • Records in a random sequence: element k is a record with probability 1/k, so E = Hₙ.

Tail-sum formula: for non-negative integer X, E[X] = Σ_{k≥1} P(X ≥ k). It's often the fastest route for maxima and waiting times: E[max of two dice] = Σ_{k=1..6} (1 − ((k − 1)/6)²) = 161/36.

The invariant and template

1. Is the quantity a count?  → write it as a sum of indicators; add their probabilities.
2. Is it a waiting time?      → split into stages of geometric waits (mean 1/p each), or use first-step analysis (13.4).
3. Is it a max/min?          → tail sums: E[X] = Σ P(X ≥ k).
4. Is it a game with choices? → work backwards: value = max(stop now, E[value of continuing]).
5. Sanity: bounds (E between min and max), small n by hand.

A thinking trace: "roll a die; you may re-roll once; you get the final value. Value of the game?"

  1. Work backwards: if you re-roll, you get a fresh roll worth 3.5.
  2. Optimal rule: keep the first roll if it beats 3.5, so keep 4, 5 or 6 and re-roll 1, 2 or 3.
  3. Value: ½ × (average of 4, 5, 6 = 5) + ½ × 3.5 = 4.25.
  4. Extend: with two re-rolls the continuation value is 4.25, so keep only 5 or 6: (2/6)(5.5) + (4/6)(4.25) = 4.67. This is optimal stopping by backward induction, the same logic as pricing an American option (Part 18).
  5. Trading framing: "what would you pay?" Answer with the fair value (4.25), then say you'd pay less for an edge, and how much risk you'd take (variance).

The habit: for sequential decisions, solve the last decision first; each earlier decision compares "stop now" with the value of the remaining game.

Traps and variations

  • E[f(X)] ≠ f(E[X]) for non-linear f (Jensen): E[X²] ≥ (E[X])².
  • E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] only if uncorrelated; linearity of sums needs nothing, but products need independence.
  • Infinite expectations: the St Petersburg game has infinite expected value but is worth little to anyone with finite wealth (utility, Kelly: Part 16).
  • Conditioning on the stopping rule: "flip until heads; expected number of tails" = 1, not ½.
  • Expected value of a ratio ≠ ratio of expected values.
