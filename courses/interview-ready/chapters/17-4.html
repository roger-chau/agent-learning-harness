The signal

"Are returns predictable?", "what's mean reversion and how fast?", "how do you cross-validate a trading model?", "what's wrong with a random train/test split here?". Financial data is a time series: ordered, autocorrelated and non-stationary. Methods that assume i.i.d. samples quietly break.

The core idea: respect time

  • Stationarity: prices are (close to) random walks, so they're non-stationary; returns are closer to stationary. Model returns, or spreads that are stationary (cointegration).
  • AR(1): xₜ = φxₜ₋₁ + εₜ. With |φ| < 1 it mean-reverts, with a half-life of ln 0.5 / ln φ and a stationary variance of σ²/(1 − φ²). φ = 1 is a random walk.
  • Autocorrelation of returns is usually tiny; the autocorrelation of squared returns (volatility clustering) is strong. That's why GARCH models volatility, and why risk changes over time.
  • Validation: never shuffle time-series data into folds. Use walk-forward validation (train on the past, test on the next block, roll forward), with a gap (\"purging\") so overlapping labels don't leak.
  • Features at time t may use only information available at t. Check every join and resample for leakage.

The invariant and template

AR(1) fit:     regress x_t on x_{t−1} → φ̂;  half-life = ln(0.5)/ln(φ̂)
Sharpe:        SR = mean(r)/std(r) · √(periods per year)     (daily: √252)
Max drawdown:  max over t of (running peak − value)/running peak   (judged problem)
Walk-forward:  for each block k: fit on [0, t_k − gap), test on [t_k, t_{k+1}); aggregate the test results

A thinking trace: "your model has 70% accuracy in cross-validation, 50% live"

  1. First suspect leakage. A live hit rate at chance level after a strong backtest almost always means information leaked in research.
  2. Random k-fold on time series: neighbouring days land in train and test; with autocorrelated features and overlapping labels (e.g. 5-day returns), the model effectively sees the test answers.
  3. Look-ahead in features: a rolling mean computed with a centred window, normalisation using full-sample statistics, or fundamentals timestamped by period rather than release date.
  4. Fix: walk-forward validation with purging and an embargo; point-in-time data; recompute every feature as of decision time.
  5. Then the remaining gap: costs, slippage, and regime change. Expect live Sharpe to be a fraction of the backtest's even when everything is done right.

Traps and variations

  • Annualising Sharpe with √252 assumes i.i.d. returns; autocorrelated returns make it misleading.
  • Overlapping returns inflate t-statistics (use Newey–West standard errors).
  • Non-stationary features (raw price levels) produce spurious fits. Use differences, returns and z-scores over rolling windows.
  • ML in finance has a low signal-to-noise ratio: simple, regularised models with strong validation usually beat complex ones.
