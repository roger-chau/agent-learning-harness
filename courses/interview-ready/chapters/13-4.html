The signal

Waiting-time questions ("expected flips until HH"), variance and risk ("what's the standard deviation of your P&L over 100 bets?"), and approximations ("roughly what's the chance of 60+ heads in 100 flips?"). The tools are first-step analysis, the law of total expectation, and the normal approximation.

The core idea: condition on the first step

Define the expected remaining time (or win probability) from each state, then write one equation per state by conditioning on what happens next. The system is small and linear.

Flips until HH: states S (nothing useful), H (last flip H). E_S = 1 + ½E_H + ½E_S and E_H = 1 + ½·0 + ½E_S, so E_S = 6. For HT: E = 4. HH takes longer because a failure from state H (a T) sends you all the way back, while for HT a failure (an H) keeps you in state H. The general answer is Σ 2^k over the pattern's self-overlaps, which is the judged problem in this chapter.

Variance: Var(X) = E[X²] − E[X]². For independent variables, variances add, so the standard deviation of a sum of n i.i.d. bets grows like √n while the mean grows like n. That's why a positive edge wins in the long run: mean / sd ∝ √n.

Distributions to know cold: Bernoulli(p) with variance p(1 − p); Binomial(n, p) with mean np and variance np(1 − p); Geometric(p) with mean 1/p; Poisson(λ) with mean and variance λ (rare events, and the limit of the binomial); Uniform(0, 1) with mean ½ and variance 1/12; Normal (68/95/99.7%).

The invariant and template

First-step analysis
  states s, terminal value at the end; for each non-terminal s:
  E_s = cost_per_step + Σ P(s → s′) · E_s′          (expected time)
  p_s = Σ P(s → s′) · p_s′                         (probability of winning)
  solve the linear system (often by hand: 2–3 states)

Normal approximation of Binomial(n, p)
  z = (k − 0.5 − np) / √(np(1−p))        (continuity correction for P(X ≥ k))

A thinking trace: "expected die rolls until two sixes in a row"

  1. States: 0 (the last roll isn't a 6), 1 (the last roll is a 6), done.
  2. Equations: E₀ = 1 + (1/6)E₁ + (5/6)E₀ and E₁ = 1 + (1/6)·0 + (5/6)E₀.
  3. Solve: from the first, E₀/6 = 1 + E₁/6, so E₀ = 6 + E₁. Substituting: E₁ = 1 + (5/6)(6 + E₁), so E₁/6 = 6 and E₁ = 36, giving E₀ = 42.
  4. Check with the pattern formula: for "66" with p = 1/6, E = 1/p + 1/p² = 6 + 36 = 42 ✓. (The coin version is 2 + 4 = 6.)
  5. Sanity: you need a 6 (about 6 rolls) and then another 6 immediately (probability 1/6), so about 6 attempts of ~7 rolls each, around 42. ✓

The habit: name the states by "what part of the goal is already achieved" (that's exactly KMP's state), then condition on one step.

Traps and variations

  • Too many states: collapse states by symmetry (only the overlap with the pattern matters).
  • Continuity correction matters for small n; for n = 100 coin flips, P(≥ 60 heads) ≈ 2.8%.
  • Poisson approximations: the chance that no one in a 365-person office shares your birthday is (364/365)³⁶⁴ ≈ e⁻¹ ≈ 0.37.
  • Variance of a sum of dependent variables needs covariances: Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) + 2Σ_{i<j}Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). For the number of fixed points of a permutation, the variance is 1.
  • Penney's game: for any pattern your opponent picks, there's a pattern that beats it more often than not. Non-transitive.
