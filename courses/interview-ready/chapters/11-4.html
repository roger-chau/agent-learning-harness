The signal

For performance engineers, the design round often becomes "design a system whose bottleneck is performance": a metrics and observability pipeline, a distributed cache, a high-throughput ingestion service, a profiling service across a fleet. These test whether you can apply Part 10's hardware intuition to whole systems.

The core idea: find the bottleneck resource, then scale or remove it

Every system has one resource that saturates first: CPU, memory bandwidth, disk IOPS, network, a lock, a single leader, a hot key. Steps:

  1. Estimate the demand on each resource from the numbers.
  2. Compare it with the per-node capacity to find the bottleneck and the node count.
  3. Remove the bottleneck: batch (amortise per-request overhead), compress (trade CPU for bandwidth), partition (split the hot resource), pre-aggregate (send less), sample (measure less).
  4. Repeat: the bottleneck moves.

Little's law: items in the system = arrival rate × time in the system. It sizes queues, thread pools and connection pools: at 10,000 requests/s and 50 ms each, ~500 are in flight.

Tail at scale: if a request fans out to 100 servers each with a 1% chance of being slow, 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of requests hit a slow server. Mitigations are hedged requests (send a backup after the p95 time), partial results, and reducing variance at each server.

The invariant and template: a metrics pipeline

agents on hosts ──(batch, compress, local pre-aggregation)──▶ ingestion tier (stateless)
   ──▶ log / queue (partitioned by series id) ──▶ aggregators (rollups: 10 s, 1 min, 1 h)
   ──▶ time-series store (columnar, delta/XOR compression) ◀── query service (+ cache)

Invariants: ingestion is append-only and idempotent (dedupe by (series, timestamp)); every stage is horizontally partitioned by series id; downsampled rollups bound query cost over long ranges; cardinality (the number of distinct series) is the scaling limit, so cap labels.

A thinking trace: sizing the metrics pipeline

  1. Requirements: 100,000 hosts × 1,000 metrics each, every 10 s; 15 days at full resolution and 1 year downsampled; dashboards query within 1 s.
  2. Ingest rate: 10⁵ × 10³ / 10 = 10⁷ points/s.
  3. Bandwidth: raw at 16 bytes/point (8-byte timestamp + 8-byte value) is 160 MB/s; with Gorilla-style delta-of-delta + XOR compression (~1.4 bytes/point) about 14 MB/s. Compression moves the bottleneck from network and disk to CPU.
  4. Storage: 10⁷ × 86,400 × 15 days × 1.4 B ≈ 18 TB (× 3 replicas), so it's sharded by series.
  5. Ingestion nodes: if one node handles ~500k points/s (parse, validate, partition), that's 20 nodes + headroom → ~30.
  6. Bottleneck check: series cardinality 10⁸ means the in-memory index (series → shard, labels) is ~10⁸ × ~100 B = 10 GB, which must be partitioned too. Query fan-out across shards brings in the tail-at-scale problem, so pre-aggregate common dashboards.

The habit: for every stage, compute demand ÷ per-node capacity, and say which resource each node runs out of first.

Traps and variations

  • Retry storms: clients retrying a struggling service multiply its load. Use exponential backoff with jitter, retry budgets and circuit breakers.
  • Synchronised behaviour: cron jobs at :00, TTLs expiring together, and clients reconnecting after an outage all cause thundering herds. Add jitter everywhere.
  • Batching trades latency for throughput: size batches by count or time, whichever comes first.
  • Observability is part of the design: name the metrics (RED: rate, errors, duration; USE: utilisation, saturation, errors) and the alerts.
  • Back to hardware: per-node capacity estimates come from Part 10 (memory bandwidth, syscalls, cache behaviour). A design that ignores them is guessing.
