The signal

Quant interviews (Jane Street, Optiver, SIG, Citadel, Five Rings, IMC, HRT…) open with probability puzzles: dice, cards, coins, random arrangements. They're testing speed and clean reasoning under pressure: can you find the slick argument, state it in one sentence, and sanity-check the number? A correct answer reached by an error-prone brute-force count still loses points against a clean symmetry argument.

The core idea: count what's easy, not what's asked

For equally likely outcomes, P(A) = |A| / |Ω|. The skill is choosing the easiest route to |A|:

  • Complement: "at least one" = 1 − P(none). P(at least one 6 in 4 rolls) = 1 − (5/6)⁴ ≈ 0.518.
  • Symmetry: if the labels are exchangeable, every label is equally likely to be first, last or largest. "The probability the ace of spades is above the king of hearts in a shuffled deck" is ½, no counting needed.
  • Sequential multiplication: deal cards one at a time and multiply conditional probabilities. P(two cards share a suit) = 12/51.
  • Inclusion–exclusion: for "at least one of several overlapping events" (derangements, coverage of all faces).
  • Choose the sample space wisely: ordered vs unordered, as long as numerator and denominator agree.

The invariant and template

1. Define the experiment and Ω (ordered or unordered? equally likely?).
2. Look for a symmetry that makes the answer immediate.
3. Otherwise pick the cheapest route: complement, sequential product, or count |A| directly.
4. Sanity checks: 0 ≤ p ≤ 1; extreme cases (n = 1, n → ∞); does a quick simulation-in-your-head agree?
5. Say the answer as a fraction AND a decimal ("5/18, about 28%").

Know these by heart: C(52, 5) = 2,598,960; the birthday problem crosses ½ at 23 people (0.507); (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368; P(no fixed point in a random permutation) → 1/e.

A thinking trace: "a random permutation of 1..n: probability that 1 and 2 are adjacent?"

  1. Counting route: glue 1 and 2 into a block: (n − 1)! orders × 2 internal orders, over n!, giving 2/n. That works, but takes care.
  2. Symmetry route: look only at where 1 and 2 land: a uniformly random pair of positions out of C(n, 2). Adjacent pairs number n − 1, so the answer is (n − 1)/C(n, 2) = 2/n.
  3. Sequential route: place 1 anywhere. With probability 2/n it's at an end (one neighbour), otherwise it has two neighbours. P = (2/n)(1/(n − 1)) + ((n − 2)/n)(2/(n − 1)) = 2/n.
  4. Sanity: n = 2 gives 1 ✓; n = 3 gives 2/3 (4 of the 6 permutations have them adjacent) ✓.

The habit: try two routes when time allows. Agreement is the best check you have in an interview, and interviewers love hearing "let me verify that another way".

Traps and variations

  • Non-equally-likely outcomes: the sums of two dice are not uniform (7 has probability 6/36).
  • Ordered vs unordered mix-ups: counting "a pair, then three others" in order but dividing by unordered hands.
  • "At least one" by adding: P(at least one 6 in 6 rolls) is not 6 × 1/6 = 1. It's 1 − (5/6)⁶ ≈ 0.665.
  • Independence assumptions: cards dealt without replacement aren't independent.
  • Speed drills: quant first rounds often give 10–20 such questions in 20 minutes. Practise in Part 15's timed drill.
