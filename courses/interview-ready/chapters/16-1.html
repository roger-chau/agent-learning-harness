The signal

"Make me a market on…" is the signature question of trading interviews (SIG, Optiver, IMC, Jane Street, Susquehanna, Citadel Securities). You name a bid (the price you'd buy at) and an ask (the price you'd sell at); the interviewer trades with you, reveals information, and watches how you update. It tests expected value, uncertainty, risk management and composure all at once.

The core idea: centre on fair value, set the width from uncertainty and risk

  • Fair value: your expected value of the thing, given everything you know.
  • Width: wide when you're uncertain or when the other side may know more (adverse selection, 16.2); tight when you're confident and want to trade. A market of 0 / 1,000,000 is useless; 49.9 / 50.1 on something you can't estimate gets you picked off.
  • Skew with your position: if you're already long, shade both prices down (you'd rather sell); if short, shade them up. This manages inventory risk.
  • Update on trades: someone buying at your ask is evidence the value is higher. Move your market up, not just your inventory.
  • Size: quote a size, e.g. "45 at 55, 10 up" (10 contracts each side). Larger size calls for a wider spread.

The invariant and template: play the game

Play at least 20 rounds of the game above. Watch three things: whether your fills come from noise traders (good, you earn the spread) or the informed trader (bad, adverse selection); how your P&L depends on your width in early rounds, when you know least; and how revealed draws should tighten your market around the new fair value. A strategy that does well on average: quote around the conditional mean, ±3–4 while two or three draws are hidden, ±1–2 once only one is.

A thinking trace: "make me a market on the sum of two dice"

  1. Fair value: 7. The distribution is symmetric and you know it exactly, so there's no information disadvantage.
  2. Width: you know the distribution perfectly, so you can be tight: "6.8 bid, at 7.2, for 10". You profit 0.2 per trade on average whichever way they trade.
  3. The interviewer buys 10 at 7.2. You're now short 10. Nothing has changed the true distribution (the buyer has no information), so keep fair value at 7, but skew to reduce risk: "6.9 / 7.3".
  4. Variation, \"one die is shown to me (not you)\": now the counterparty is informed. Widen a lot, "5.5 / 8.5", because they'll only trade when your price is wrong for the die they've seen.
  5. Variation, \"you see one die: it's a 6\": fair value 6 + 3.5 = 9.5, and you're the informed side now. Quote tightly around 9.5.

The habit: say your fair value and why, then your width and why ("7 fair; tight because the distribution is known and you have no information edge").

Traps and variations

  • Crossed or locked markets (bid ≥ ask) are free money for the other side. Never.
  • Refusing to quote is the worst answer. Quote wide, then tighten.
  • Anchoring: when information arrives, recompute fair value from scratch; don't nudge.
  • Ignoring position: after several buys from you, a large short position is a risk even at fair value.
  • Composure: interviewers will trade aggressively against you on purpose. Narrate calmly: \"you've bought three times; either you know something or you're testing me; I'll widen and move up.\"
