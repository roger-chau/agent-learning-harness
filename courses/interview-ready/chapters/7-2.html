The signal

n is tiny, at most about 20 (often 12–16), and the problem involves which items are used: visit every node, assign every task to a worker, partition into groups, choose a subset with pairwise constraints. A subset of n ≤ 20 items fits in an integer mask, and 2²⁰ ≈ 10⁶ masks is affordable. The constraint "n ≤ 15" in an online assessment is often the problem telling you the intended complexity.

The core idea: the future depends on the set, not the order

Permutation problems look like n! work. The DP insight is that the remaining problem depends only on which items are used (and maybe where you are now), not on the order you used them in. So the state is (mask) or (mask, last), and n! collapses to 2ⁿ or n·2ⁿ.

Enumerating the submasks of every mask costs 3ⁿ in total (each item is in s, in m but not s, or outside m). That's about 3.5 × 10⁹ for n = 20, so it only works for n ≤ 15 or so.

The invariant and template

# Held–Karp shape: dp[mask][v] = best way to have visited exactly mask, ending at v
dp = [[INF] * n for _ in range(1 << n)]
for s in starts: dp[1 << s][s] = 0
for mask in range(1 << n):                 # increasing masks: every subset comes before its supersets
    for u in range(n):
        if dp[mask][u] == INF: continue
        for v in range(n):
            if not mask >> v & 1:
                nm = mask | 1 << v
                dp[nm][v] = min(dp[nm][v], dp[mask][u] + w(u, v))

Order: a superset is numerically larger than its subsets, so ascending masks is a valid topological order. When transitions all cost 1, BFS over (node, mask) does the same job (shortest path visiting all nodes).

A thinking trace: partition to k equal-sum subsets

  1. Necessary checks: the sum must be divisible by k, and no element may exceed the target t. These are cheap early outs.
  2. Backtracking: fill k buckets, trying each number in each bucket. That's kⁿ, though pruning (sort descending, skip identical empty buckets) helps a lot.
  3. Look for the state: does the arrangement of numbers into buckets matter? If we fill buckets one at a time, the only thing that matters is the set of numbers used. The current bucket's fill is then (sum of mask) mod t, determined by the mask.
  4. DP: reachable[mask], extending by any unused number that fits in the current bucket. The answer is reachable[full]. That's O(2ⁿ · n), which is 10⁶ for n = 16.
  5. Test: k = 1 (always true), an element greater than t (false), and many duplicates.

The move: impose an order (fill buckets one at a time) so the state shrinks to a set. Canonical orderings remove the symmetry that makes brute force explode.

Traps and variations

  • Operator precedence: in C++, m & 1 << i parses as m & (1 << i), but m >> i & 1 == 0 parses as m >> i & (1 == 0). Parenthesise.
  • Memory: 2²⁰ × 20 ints is 80 MB. Check before declaring dp[1 << n][n].
  • Assignment problems (n workers, n jobs): dp[mask] where popcount(mask) is the next worker. That's O(2ⁿ · n) and needs no second index.
  • Profile DP (broken profile, tiling a grid with dominoes): the mask is the boundary between filled and unfilled cells.
  • SOS DP (sum over subsets): computes Σ over all submasks for every mask in O(2ⁿ · n), not 3ⁿ.
  • When n ≈ 40: meet in the middle (2 × 2²⁰), not a 2⁴⁰ mask DP.
