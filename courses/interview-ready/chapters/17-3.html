The signal

"You tested 100 strategies and the best has a Sharpe of 2. Is it real?", "what's a p-value?", "how do you avoid overfitting a backtest?". The biggest practical risk in quant research is fooling yourself, and interviewers probe it directly.

The core idea: the more you search, the more impressive luck looks

  • p-value: P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis). It is not P(null | data).
  • Power: P(rejecting the null | a real effect of a given size). Small samples have low power, and significant results from them overestimate the effect size.
  • Multiple testing: with m independent tests at level α, P(at least one false positive) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ. With 20 tests at 5%, that's 64%. Bonferroni tests each at α/m; the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate.
  • Selection bias in Sharpe ratios: the maximum of N zero-skill strategies' Sharpe ratios grows like σ_SR·√(2 ln N). Deflate the best Sharpe by the number of trials you ran (the deflated Sharpe ratio).
  • Sharpe significance: with an annualised Sharpe SR over T years, t ≈ SR·√T. A Sharpe of 1 needs about 4 years for t ≈ 2.

The invariant and template

Before testing:  write down the hypothesis, the test, and the number of variants you'll try.
Hold out:       a test set you touch ONCE (or walk-forward periods), never used to choose parameters.
Adjust:         for the number of trials (Bonferroni/BH, deflated Sharpe).
Report:         effect size + SE + the number of trials, not just "p < 0.05".
Sanity:         does the effect survive costs, different periods, different markets, small parameter changes?

A thinking trace: "best of 100 backtests has Sharpe 2.0 over 3 years"

  1. Noise in a Sharpe estimate: SE ≈ √(1/T) ≈ 0.58 for T = 3 years (ignoring higher moments).
  2. Expected max under zero skill: about 0.58 × √(2 ln 100) ≈ 0.58 × 3.03 ≈ 1.76. A best-of-100 Sharpe of 2.0 is only slightly above what pure luck delivers.
  3. Conclusion: weak evidence. The strategy needs out-of-sample confirmation: fresh data, other markets, or live paper trading.
  4. Further checks: parameter stability (does Sharpe collapse when a lookback moves from 20 to 22 days?), transaction costs, capacity, and economic rationale (why should this work?).

The habit: always ask how many things were tried before this one was shown to you, including by other researchers and in published papers.

Traps and variations

  • Look-ahead bias: using data not available at decision time (restated fundamentals, close prices for open trades, survivor-only universes).
  • Survivorship bias: testing only on stocks that exist today inflates returns.
  • p-hacking: trying specifications until p < 0.05; garden of forking paths.
  • \"Statistically significant\" ≠ economically significant: a tiny edge may not survive costs.
  • Regime changes: a stationarity assumption hides behind every backtest.
