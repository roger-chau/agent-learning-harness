The signal

"Derive the OLS estimator", "what does R² tell you?", "two highly correlated features: what happens to the coefficients?", "why regularise?". Regression is the workhorse of quantitative research: factor models, signal combination, hedge ratios.

The core idea: projection

OLS minimises Σ(y − Xβ)², giving β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy: the projection of y onto the column space of X. With one regressor: b = Cov(x, y)/Var(x) = ρ·σ_y/σ_x, and a = ȳ − b x̄. The judged problem in this chapter implements exactly this, stably.

  • R² = the fraction of variance explained (= ρ² with one regressor). In finance, an R² of 0.01 for predicting returns can be very valuable; don't dismiss small R².
  • Coefficient SE: σ_ε / (√n · σ_x) for one regressor. Collinear regressors inflate the SEs (the variance inflation factor), so coefficients become unstable even when predictions are fine.
  • Omitted variable bias: if y depends on z, z is left out, and z correlates with x, then b absorbs z's effect: E[b̂] = β + γ·Cov(x, z)/Var(x).
  • Regularisation: ridge (L2) shrinks coefficients and stabilises collinear fits; lasso (L1) sets some exactly to zero (feature selection). Choose λ by cross-validation.
  • Hedge ratio: regressing an asset's returns on a hedge instrument's returns gives the minimum-variance hedge ratio b = ρ σ_asset / σ_hedge.

The invariant and template

One regressor:   b = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)²,   a = ȳ − b·x̄,   R² = ρ²
Many:            β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀ y     (solve with QR/Cholesky; never invert explicitly)
Ridge:           β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹ Xᵀ y
Assumptions to state: linearity, exogeneity (E[ε | X] = 0), homoskedastic and independent errors (for the textbook SEs)

A thinking trace: "your signal's regression coefficient flipped sign when you added a feature"

  1. Setup: regressing returns on momentum gives b = +0.3. Adding volatility makes momentum's coefficient −0.1.
  2. Hypothesis 1, omitted variable bias: momentum correlates with volatility, and volatility predicts returns. The first regression attributed volatility's effect to momentum.
  3. Hypothesis 2, collinearity: if momentum and volatility are highly correlated, the individual coefficients have huge SEs, and the sign flip may be noise. Check the SEs and the VIF.
  4. Diagnose: correlation between the features; the coefficient SEs before and after; out-of-sample prediction with each model.
  5. Act: if the goal is prediction, regularise and judge by out-of-sample error; if the goal is attribution, accept that the data can't separate collinear effects.

Traps and variations

  • Regressing on a variable that contains y (or is built from future data) gives a spuriously high R².
  • Two random walks regressed on each other show a \"significant\" relation (spurious regression). Use returns, or test for cointegration.
  • Outliers dominate least squares: robust regression (Huber), or winsorise.
  • Errors-in-variables: noise in x biases b toward zero (attenuation).
  • Numerics: centre the data; QR rather than normal equations (the condition number gets squared).
