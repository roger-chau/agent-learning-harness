The signal

"Price an option in a one-step binomial model", "why doesn't the real-world probability matter?", "what's risk-neutral pricing?", "price an American put". The binomial model contains all of derivatives pricing in miniature: replication, no-arbitrage, risk-neutral probabilities and backward induction.

The core idea: replicate, and the price is forced

A stock at S goes to Su or Sd. An option pays V_u or V_d. Hold Δ shares and B in bonds so the portfolio matches the option in both states:

Δ = (V_u − V_d)/(Su − Sd), and the price is ΔS + B

Any other price is an arbitrage. Rearranged, the price is e^{−rΔt}[q V_u + (1 − q) V_d] with q = (e^{rΔt} − d)/(u − d), the risk-neutral probability. The real probability of an up move never appears: it's already in the stock price, and the option is priced relative to the stock.

Multi-step trees apply this backward from expiry. American options take max(exercise, continuation) at each node: the judged problem. As the steps shrink, the CRR tree converges to Black–Scholes.

The invariant and template

One step:    q = (e^{rΔt} − d)/(u − d)      (needs d < e^{rΔt} < u: no-arbitrage)
             V = e^{−rΔt} [q V_u + (1 − q) V_d]
             Δ = (V_u − V_d) / (S u − S d)
CRR tree:    u = e^{σ√Δt}, d = 1/u;  backward induction;  American: max(payoff now, continuation)
Put–call parity (European, no dividends):   C − P = S − K e^{−rT}

A thinking trace: one-step call

S = 100 goes to 120 or 80 in one year; r = 0; call with K = 100.

  1. Payoffs: V_u = 20, V_d = 0.
  2. Replicate: Δ = (20 − 0)/(120 − 80) = 0.5 shares; at the bottom 0.5 × 80 = 40 must be offset to pay 0, so B = −40. Price = 0.5 × 100 − 40 = 10.
  3. Risk-neutral check: q = (1 − 0.8)/(1.2 − 0.8) = 0.5, so V = 0.5 × 20 = 10 ✓.
  4. Real-world probability of the up move (say 90%) doesn't change the price. If you sold the call for more than 10 and replicated, you'd lock in the difference whatever happens.
  5. Put via parity: P = C − S + K = 10 − 100 + 100 = 10 (r = 0).

Traps and variations

  • Using real probabilities to price is the most common conceptual error.
  • Early exercise: American calls on non-dividend stocks are never exercised early; American puts sometimes are (deep in the money, positive rates).
  • Dividends make early exercise of calls possible (just before the ex-dividend date).
  • Tree convergence oscillates with odd/even steps; averaging adjacent step counts helps.
  • Arbitrage checks: a call can't be worth more than the stock or less than max(S − Ke^{−rT}, 0).
