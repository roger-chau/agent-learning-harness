The signal

Different employers listen for different things. Big tech often uses explicit competencies (Amazon's Leadership Principles, Google's \"Googleyness\" and leadership attributes, Meta's focus on impact); trading firms probe decision-making under uncertainty, intellectual honesty and how you handle being wrong; hardware and AI companies probe technical passion and depth. \"Why us?\" is asked everywhere.

The core idea: same stories, different emphasis

Employer typeListening forEmphasise in your stories
Big techownership, impact at scale, collaboration, dealing with ambiguityscope, measurable impact, cross-team work
Trading firmsclear thinking under uncertainty, owning mistakes fast, competitiveness without egodecisions with incomplete information, quantified risk, admitting errors
GPU / AI hardware & infradepth, curiosity about the stack, performance obsessionmeasurements, first-principles reasoning, self-driven learning
Startupsspeed, breadth, comfort with chaosshipping, wearing many hats, judgement on what not to build

\"Why us?\": a specific answer naming the team's work (a paper, a product, an open-source project, a talk), how it connects to what you've done, and what you want to learn there. Generic praise (\"great culture\") scores nothing.

Your questions for them are part of the interview: ask about the team's current hardest problem, how success is measured in the first six months, and how engineers and researchers or traders work together.

The invariant and template: a \"why us\" answer

1. Specific hook:  "Your team's work on X (the 2025 talk on Y / the Z library) ..."
2. Connection:     "... is exactly the problem I've been working on: I took a GEMM kernel from A to B ..."
3. Want:           "I want to work where performance is the product, and learn W from people who build it at scale."
(≈ 45 seconds)

A thinking trace: \"tell me about a time you were wrong\" (trading firm)

  1. What they're testing: do you update quickly when evidence contradicts you, and own it without defensiveness? It's exactly the market-making skill from Part 16.
  2. Choose a story where you held a confident belief, evidence showed it was wrong, you changed course fast, and the change helped.
  3. Tell it with probabilities: \"I was fairly sure the slowdown was compute-bound. The roofline measurement put it squarely in the memory-bound region, so I dropped the compute optimisation after one experiment and fixed the access pattern instead.\"
  4. Close with the lesson as a behaviour: \"Now I measure before choosing an optimisation, and I set a kill criterion before I start an experiment.\"

Traps and variations

  • Not researching the company: fifteen minutes on their engineering blog changes the conversation.
  • Rambling: answer in about two minutes, then stop and let them probe.
  • No questions at the end signals low interest.
  • Inconsistent stories across interviewers (they compare notes).
