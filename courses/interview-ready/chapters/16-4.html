The signal

"We play a game: what's your strategy?", "how should you bluff?", "guess two-thirds of the average", "what's the equilibrium?". Trading is a repeated game against other strategic players, and quant interviews test whether you reason about the opponent's best response rather than just your own EV.

The core idea: best responses and indifference

  • Nash equilibrium: each player's strategy is a best response to the others'. Nobody gains by deviating alone.
  • Dominance first: eliminate strategies that are worse in every case; iterate.
  • Mixed strategies via indifference: in a zero-sum game with no saddle point, each player mixes so that the opponent is indifferent between their options. Otherwise the opponent exploits the bias.
  • Backward induction for sequential games (Part 15.4's pirates).
  • Bluffing frequency: in a simple poker model, bet your strong hands and bluff with enough weak hands that the caller's EV of calling is zero: bluff ratio = pot odds. With a pot of P and a bet of B, the caller needs to win B/(P + 2B) of the time to break even, so the bettor bluffs in that proportion of their betting range.

The invariant and template

2×2 zero-sum, row payoffs [[a, b], [c, d]] with no saddle point:
  row plays row 1 with p where  a p + c (1−p) = b p + d (1−p)
  p = (d − c) / (a − b − c + d),   value v = a p + c (1−p)
Checks: p ∈ [0, 1]; plugging into both columns gives the same v.

A thinking trace: "guess 2/3 of the average"

  1. Rules: everyone picks a number in [0, 100]; the closest to ⅔ of the average wins.
  2. Dominance: the average can't exceed 100, so ⅔ of it can't exceed 66.7, and any pick above 66.7 is dominated.
  3. Iterate: if nobody picks above 66.7, the target is at most 44.4; then 29.6; … the only Nash equilibrium is 0.
  4. But play the room: real groups stop after 1–2 levels of reasoning; winning answers are typically 20–35. The equilibrium and the profit-maximising answer differ when the others aren't fully rational.
  5. Trading lesson: markets reward modelling what others will actually do, not what perfectly rational agents would do.

Traps and variations

  • Confusing \"maximise my payoff\" with \"make the opponent indifferent\": in mixed equilibria, your mix is chosen to neutralise the opponent.
  • Auctions: in a first-price sealed-bid auction with n bidders and uniform private values, bid (n − 1)/n of your value; second-price, bid your value (truthful).
  • Repeated games sustain cooperation (tit-for-tat) that one-shot games don't.
  • Interview games (\"I pick a number 1–100, you guess, I say higher or lower, you pay per guess\"): compute your strategy's expected cost and then think about the setter's best response.
