The signal

"How would you estimate p from these flips?", "what's the standard error of your average?", "how many samples do you need?", "is this estimator biased?". Quant researcher interviews (and quant-dev ones at research-heavy firms) test whether you can turn data into an estimate with an honest uncertainty, the core of every trading signal.

The core idea: an estimate is worthless without its standard error

  • Maximum likelihood: choose the parameter that makes the observed data most probable. Maximise the log-likelihood; set its derivative to zero. Bernoulli: p̂ = k/n. Exponential: λ̂ = 1/x̄. Normal: μ̂ = x̄, σ̂² = (1/n)Σ(x − x̄)², which is biased low; the unbiased version divides by n − 1.
  • Standard error of a mean: σ/√n. Halving the error needs 4× the data. For a proportion: √(p(1 − p)/n).
  • Central limit theorem: sums and means of many independent terms are approximately normal, so a 95% interval is about estimate ± 1.96 SE.
  • Bias–variance: MSE = bias² + variance. A slightly biased estimator with much lower variance can win (shrinkage, regularisation).
  • Bootstrap: when the formula for the SE is hard, resample the data with replacement, recompute the statistic, and use the spread of the results.

The invariant and template

1. Model: what distribution generated the data? What's the parameter?
2. Estimate: MLE (or method of moments) → θ̂.
3. Uncertainty: SE from a formula (σ/√n, √(p(1−p)/n), Fisher information) or the bootstrap.
4. Interval: θ̂ ± 1.96·SE (if approximately normal).
5. Sanity: is n large enough? Are the observations independent? (Correlated data → fewer effective samples.)

A thinking trace: "is this coin biased? 60 heads in 100"

  1. Estimate: p̂ = 0.60.
  2. SE: √(0.6 × 0.4 / 100) ≈ 0.049. A 95% interval is 0.60 ± 0.096, about [0.50, 0.70], so it barely includes 0.5.
  3. Test of p = 0.5: under the null, SE = 0.05, so z = 0.10/0.05 = 2.0, a two-sided p-value ≈ 0.046. Borderline.
  4. Sample size to detect p = 0.55 reliably: you need the difference (0.05) to be about 2.8 SE (for 5% significance with 80% power), so SE ≈ 0.018 and n ≈ 0.25 / 0.018² ≈ 780 flips.
  5. Trading framing: a strategy with a true 55% hit rate needs hundreds of trades before you can tell it from luck. That's why researchers are sceptical of short track records.

The habit: always pair an estimate with its standard error, and ask how many samples you'd need.

Traps and variations

  • Autocorrelated data: daily returns of an overlapping-window signal aren't independent; the effective n is smaller and the SE larger.
  • Fat tails: the CLT converges slowly for heavy-tailed returns; the sample variance is noisy.
  • The n − 1 correction matters for small samples only.
  • MLE of a uniform's upper bound is the sample max (biased low); the unbiased fix is max × (n + 1)/n. This is the classic \"German tank problem\".
