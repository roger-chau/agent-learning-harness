The signal

"You have an edge: how much of your bankroll do you bet?", "why don't traders bet full Kelly?", "a 60% coin at even money: how should you size?". Position sizing turns a positive-EV idea into long-run growth instead of ruin. Firms ask because traders who size badly blow up even with an edge.

The core idea: maximise expected log wealth

For repeated bets where you reinvest, the long-run growth rate of wealth is E[log(1 + f·R)], where f is the fraction bet and R the return. Maximising it gives the Kelly criterion. For a bet paying b-to-1 with win probability p:

f* = p − q/b (q = 1 − p): "edge over odds"

At even money (b = 1): f* = 2p − 1, so a 60% coin calls for betting 20%. For continuous returns with mean μ and variance σ² (excess over the risk-free rate): f* ≈ μ/σ².

  • Over-betting is far worse than under-betting: the growth rate is roughly parabolic in f, peaks at f*, falls to 0 at about 2f*, and goes negative beyond that, so ruin is nearly certain even with an edge.
  • Half Kelly keeps about ¾ of the growth rate with about half the volatility, and it's robust to overestimating your edge, which you will. Practitioners use fractional Kelly.

The invariant and template

Binary bet (win b per 1 staked w.p. p):   f* = p − (1 − p)/b
Growth rate:   g(f) = p·ln(1 + b f) + (1 − p)·ln(1 − f)
Small-edge approximation:   g(f) ≈ f·μ − f²σ²/2   →  f* = μ/σ²,  g* = μ²/(2σ²) = Sharpe²/2
Rule of thumb: bet a fraction of Kelly (½ or less); never more than Kelly.

A thinking trace: the 60% coin

  1. EV per $1 bet at even money: 0.6 − 0.4 = $0.20. Positive.
  2. Bet everything each time? The expected wealth is maximised, but one loss wipes you out, and you will lose eventually. The median outcome is ruin. Expectation is the wrong objective for repeated reinvestment.
  3. Kelly: f* = 0.6 − 0.4 = 0.2. Growth rate g = 0.6 ln 1.2 + 0.4 ln 0.8 ≈ 0.020 per bet, so wealth grows about e^{0.02n}: roughly 7× in 100 bets.
  4. Half Kelly (10%): g = 0.6 ln 1.1 + 0.4 ln 0.9 ≈ 0.015, which is 75% of the growth with much smaller drawdowns.
  5. 40% (double Kelly): g = 0.6 ln 1.4 + 0.4 ln 0.6 ≈ −0.002. Negative growth, despite a 20% edge.

The habit: for repeated bets, optimise growth (log), not expectation, then shade down for estimation error.

Traps and variations

  • Kelly needs your true edge. Estimated edges are noisy and usually optimistic, which is the main reason to use fractional Kelly.
  • Several simultaneous bets need joint optimisation (correlations matter).
  • Kelly is about growth, not utility: institutions with drawdown limits use smaller fractions.
  • Negative edge: f* < 0 means take the other side, or don't bet.
  • Time to recover: a 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain. Asymmetry is why sizing dominates long-run results.
