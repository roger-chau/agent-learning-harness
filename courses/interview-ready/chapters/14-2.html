The signal

"A gambler with $i bets until $0 or $N…", "expected time for a random walk to hit ±a", "a fair game: can a stopping strategy beat it?". Martingales give the shortest proofs for random-walk questions, and the idea that no stopping rule beats a fair game is the mathematical heart of market efficiency and option pricing.

The core idea: fair games stay fair under (reasonable) stopping

A martingale Mₙ satisfies E[Mₙ₊₁ | history] = Mₙ: your expected future wealth equals your current wealth. Optional stopping: for a bounded stopping time (or a bounded martingale), E[M_τ] = M₀. To use it:

  1. Find a martingale built from the process.
  2. Apply E[M_τ] = M₀ at the stopping time.
  3. Solve for the unknown probability or expectation.
  • Fair gambler's ruin: wealth Sₙ is a martingale, so i = N·P(win) + 0·P(lose), giving P(reach N) = i/N.
  • Expected duration: Sₙ² − n is a martingale, so E[S_τ²] − E[τ] = i², giving E[τ] = N·i − i² = i(N − i).
  • Biased walk (p ≠ q): (q/p)^{Sₙ} is a martingale, giving P(reach N) = (1 − (q/p)ⁱ)/(1 − (q/p)ᴺ).

The invariant and template

Simple random walk from 0, stop at +a or −b:
  P(hit +a first) = b / (a + b)
  E[time]         = a · b
Biased (p up, q = 1 − p), from i, absorb at 0 and N:
  P(hit N) = (1 − (q/p)^i) / (1 − (q/p)^N)
Martingales to try: S_n − n(p − q),   S_n² − n (fair),   (q/p)^{S_n},   e^{θS_n} / (E e^{θX})^n

A thinking trace: "can a doubling strategy beat roulette?"

  1. Strategy (martingale betting): bet $1 on red; after a loss, double; stop after the first win. Each "round" nets +$1 with high probability.
  2. Catch 1, fair game: wealth is a martingale. If your bankroll is finite (you stop when you can't double), optional stopping applies and E[final wealth] = initial wealth. The many small wins are exactly paid for by rare catastrophic losses.
  3. Quantify: with a $1,023 bankroll (10 doublings) on a fair coin, you lose $1,023 with probability 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 0.001 and win $1 otherwise: 0.999 × 1 − 0.001 × 1,023 ≈ 0.
  4. Catch 2, real roulette: p = 18/38 < ½, so wealth is a supermartingale: the expected value is negative and the strategy only reshapes the distribution (frequent small wins, rare ruin).
  5. Trading lesson: strategies with a high win rate and a fat left tail (selling deep out-of-the-money options) can look brilliant for years. Judge a strategy by its expected value and tail risk, not its hit rate.

Traps and variations

  • Optional stopping needs conditions: "stop when ahead by $1" with an unlimited bankroll and time has E[τ] = ∞, and E[M_τ] = 1 ≠ 0. The theorem's conditions matter.
  • Ballot problem / reflection: P(the winner leads throughout the count) = (a − b)/(a + b).
  • Wald's identity: E[S_τ] = E[τ]·E[X] for i.i.d. steps and a valid stopping time. Expected rolls to accumulate a sum ≥ k ≈ k/3.5.
  • Doob's maximal inequality and Kelly (Part 16) are the next steps when risk of ruin, not just expectation, matters.
