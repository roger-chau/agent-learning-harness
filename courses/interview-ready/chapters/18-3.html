The signal

"Write down Black–Scholes", "what's the delta of an at-the-money call?", "which Greek is largest near expiry?", "price a digital option". Black–Scholes is the shared vocabulary of every options desk; interviews test both the formula and the intuition behind each term.

The core idea: hedge away the randomness, and what's left earns the risk-free rate

A call hedged with −Δ shares is instantaneously riskless (Itô: the dW terms cancel when Δ = ∂V/∂S), so it must earn r. That gives the PDE ∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²∂²V/∂S² + rS∂V/∂S − rV = 0, solved for a European call by

C = S N(d₁) − K e^{−rT} N(d₂), d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T), d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

Reading the terms: N(d₂) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money; S N(d₁) is the present value of receiving the stock if it does. A digital call (pays 1 if S_T > K) is therefore worth e^{−rT}N(d₂).

The invariant and template

At the money, r ≈ 0:   C ≈ P ≈ 0.4 · S · σ√T        (since φ(0) = 1/√(2π) ≈ 0.4)
Put:                   P = K e^{−rT} N(−d₂) − S N(−d₁)     (or by parity)
Theta–gamma:           Θ + ½ σ² S² Γ = r(V − S Δ)    (r ≈ 0: Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ)
Delta of a put:        N(d₁) − 1

A thinking trace: "quick, price an ATM one-year call, S = 100, σ = 20%"

  1. Rule of thumb (r ≈ 0): 0.4 × 100 × 0.2 × 1 = 8.
  2. With r = 5%: the forward is higher, so the call is worth more. The exact value is ≈ 10.45 (the judged problem's first test).
  3. Delta: d₁ = (0 + 0.07)/0.2 = 0.35, N(0.35) ≈ 0.64. Above ½ because of the drift from the rate and the σ²/2 term.
  4. A sanity bound: C ≥ S − Ke^{−rT} = 100 − 95.1 = 4.9 ✓, and C ≤ S ✓.
  5. Vol sensitivity: vega = 100 × φ(0.35) × 1 ≈ 37.5, so +1 vol point (0.01) adds about $0.375.

The habit: rule of thumb first, then bounds, then the exact formula. On a desk the first two are what you use in real time.

Traps and variations

  • Units: σ annualised and T in years; vega quoted per 1 vol point (÷ 100).
  • Delta isn't the probability of exercise (that's N(d₂) under Q), though they're close for short-dated options.
  • Gamma near expiry blows up at the money: a hedging nightmare (pin risk).
  • Black–Scholes assumptions (constant σ, continuous hedging, no jumps, lognormal returns) all fail in practice; the market corrects with the volatility smile (18.4).
