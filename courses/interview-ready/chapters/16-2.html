The signal

"Why do market makers quote a spread at all?", "you win an auction for a jar of coins: should you be happy?", "a customer wants to buy 10× normal size: what do you do?". Adverse selection, trading against someone who knows more than you, is the central risk of market making and the reason spreads exist.

The core idea: every fill is evidence

When someone trades with you, ask: "given that they chose to trade, what's the value now?" The Glosten–Milgrom model makes this exact. The value is high (H) or low (L) with equal probability; a fraction μ of traders know it and buy if H, sell if L; the rest buy or sell at random. A competitive market maker sets

ask = E[V | a buy arrives], bid = E[V | a sell arrives]

So the spread exists even with zero costs, and it widens with μ, the share of informed flow: with H = 100, L = 0, the spread is exactly 100μ. The market maker loses to the informed traders and wins it back from the uninformed. The spread is the price of that information asymmetry.

The winner's curse: in a common-value auction, winning means you had the highest estimate, which is probably an overestimate. Bid below your estimate, more so with more bidders.

The invariant and template

Before quoting:  fair = E[V | public info]
On a buy at ask: fair ← E[V | public info, "someone chose to buy at ask"]  (higher)
Quote:           ask = E[V | buy], bid = E[V | sell]   (regret-free prices)
Glosten–Milgrom (V ∈ {L, H} with ½ each, informed fraction μ):
                 ask = L + (H − L)·(1 + μ)/2,   bid = L + (H − L)·(1 − μ)/2,   spread = μ(H − L)

A thinking trace: the large customer order

  1. Setup: you make markets in a stock at 50.00 / 50.02 for 1,000 shares. A client asks to buy 100,000 shares now.
  2. Ask what the size tells you: large, urgent orders are more likely to be informed (they know news) or to come from a client who'll keep buying (price pressure). Either way, the price is likely to rise after you sell.
  3. Price the risk: you'll be short 100k shares and need to buy them back. Estimate the market impact of buying 100k (say 10–20 cents given typical daily volume) plus the information risk.
  4. Quote: offer at, say, 50.15 for the block, or offer to work the order over time (agency) instead of taking the risk (principal).
  5. Learn: track post-trade price moves (\"mark-outs\") by client. Clients whose trades are consistently followed by adverse moves get wider prices.

The habit: condition on the fact that the trade happened. The question isn't \"what's it worth?\" but \"what's it worth given that this person wants to trade it with me now?\"

Traps and variations

  • Winning every trade is a warning sign: if you're always filled, your prices are too generous.
  • Toxic vs benign flow: retail order flow is mostly uninformed, which is why market makers pay for it.
  • Kyle's model: an informed trader splits the order over time to hide; the price impact per unit (λ) reflects how informed the flow is.
  • Interview games: when the interviewer \"knows\" a card or a die, assume any trade they make is informed, and widen.
