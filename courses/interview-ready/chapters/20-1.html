The signal

Every loop has at least one behavioural conversation, and in many it decides between two technically similar candidates: "tell me about a time you…", "your hardest bug", "a conflict with a teammate", "a project you're proud of", "a failure". Interviewers are checking ownership, judgement, communication and how you learn, and they can only judge what you say, so a prepared story bank isn't fakery. It's making sure your real experience comes across.

The core idea: six stories cover sixty questions

Most behavioural questions map onto a handful of themes. Prepare six stories, each rich enough to answer several themes:

StoryCovers
1. A hard technical problem you solved (ideally performance)hardest bug, technical depth, persistence, proudest work
2. A failure or mistake and what changed afterwardsfailure, learning, ownership, feedback
3. A disagreement you handledconflict, influence without authority, communication
4. Something you led or initiatedleadership, initiative, ambiguity, prioritisation
5. A tight deadline or a trade-off under pressureprioritisation, judgement, scope cuts, delivery
6. Learning something hard quicklycuriosity, growth (your GPU self-study is a strong candidate)

Tell each in STAR form, Situation, Task, Action, Result, with most of the time on Action (what you did and why) and a measurable Result (latency down 3.2×, a bug class eliminated, a team unblocked a week early). End with a short reflection: what you'd do differently.

The invariant and template

Title:      "Halved inference latency by fixing a memory-bound kernel"
Situation:  one or two sentences of context (team, system, stakes)
Task:       what you were responsible for
Action:     3–5 concrete steps YOU took, including how you decided (measurements, alternatives rejected)
Result:     numbers + impact on people or the business
Reflection: what you learned / would do differently
Themes:     hard problem, ownership, data-driven
Length:     about 2 minutes spoken (≈ 250–300 words)

Write your six stories in the box below (the write-up saves with your progress). Then tell each aloud to a timer and cut anything that isn't Action or Result.

A thinking trace: turning "I worked on GPU stuff" into a story

  1. Vague version: "I learned CUDA and optimised some kernels." Nothing to evaluate.
  2. Find the decision points: what was slow, how you found out (profiler, roofline), what you tried first, what didn't work, what did, and why.
  3. Add numbers: baseline 4 TFLOP/s, 31 TFLOP/s after shared-memory tiling, 78% of the measured peak; bank conflicts eliminated (from Nsight's shared-memory metrics).
  4. Add the human element: the constraint you worked within, what you'd tell a teammate, what surprised you.
  5. Result: "Taking a GEMM from 4 to 31 TFLOP/s taught me to measure before guessing. My first idea (more threads) did nothing because the kernel was memory-bound, which the roofline made obvious."

The habit: every story needs a decision, a measurement and a number. Interviewers probe exactly those three.

Traps and variations

  • \"We\" everywhere: interviewers want your contribution. Say \"I\" for what you did, and credit others specifically.
  • No result, or results without numbers.
  • Fake failures (\"I work too hard\"). Pick a real mistake with a real lesson and a changed behaviour.
  • Badmouthing former colleagues or employers, even when justified.
  • Over-rehearsed delivery: know the beats, not a script.
