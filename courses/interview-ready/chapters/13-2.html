The signal

"Given that…", "a test comes back positive…", "you see heads; what's the chance the coin is biased?", Monty Hall and the two-child problem. Conditioning questions are designed to trigger intuitive wrong answers. Trading is, at its core, updating beliefs on evidence, which is why quant firms ask so many.

The core idea: Bayes in odds form

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B). The fastest form for interviews is odds:

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio

Disease with 1% prevalence, test with 99% sensitivity and a 5% false-positive rate: prior odds 1:99, likelihood ratio 0.99/0.05 = 19.8, posterior odds 19.8:99 ≈ 1:5, so P(disease | +) ≈ 1/6. With independent evidence, multiply the likelihood ratios: a second positive test makes it 19.8 × 19.8 : 99 ≈ 4:1.

The other workhorse is a tree or table of natural frequencies: "imagine 10,000 people…". It's harder to get wrong under pressure than the formula.

The invariant and template

1. Write the hypotheses H₁…Hₖ and their priors.
2. Write P(evidence | Hᵢ) for each, using exactly the information revealed,
   including HOW it was revealed (the host's rule, the sampling process).
3. Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood; normalise.
4. Sanity: does stronger evidence move the posterior the right way and by a sensible amount?

Step 2's "how it was revealed" is where Monty Hall and the two-child problem live. The protocol that generated the information is part of the evidence.

A thinking trace: "two children, at least one is a boy born on a Tuesday"

  1. Baseline: "at least one boy" gives P(two boys) = 1/3 (BB out of BB, BG, GB).
  2. Model the extra detail: each child is (sex, day): 14 equally likely types; there are 14² = 196 ordered pairs.
  3. Count the evidence: pairs with at least one Tuesday-boy: 196 − 13² = 27.
  4. Count both boys among them: boy pairs: 7² = 49, minus those with no Tuesday-boy, 6² = 36, giving 13.
  5. Answer: 13/27 ≈ 0.48, close to ½. The more specific the identifying detail, the closer the answer gets to ½, because it nearly picks out one particular child.
  6. Protocol caveat: this assumes the statement means "a family selected because it has at least one Tuesday-boy". If the parent just mentioned one child at random, the answer is ½. Say which model you're using.

The habit: enumerate the joint outcomes and condition by filtering. When intuition and counting disagree, counting wins, and the disagreement usually reveals a hidden assumption about the protocol.

Traps and variations

  • Base-rate neglect: ignoring the prior (the 1% prevalence).
  • Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) (the prosecutor's fallacy).
  • Monty Hall: switching wins 2/3 because the host knowingly avoids the car. If the host opens a door at random and happens to show a goat, it's ½.
  • Independent vs conditionally independent: two coin flips from an unknown coin are dependent, since the first flip tells you about the coin.
  • Bertrand's box / the double-headed coin: after seeing heads, the double-headed coin is twice as likely (it has two heads that could be showing).
